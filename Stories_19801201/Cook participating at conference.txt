Cook participating at conference

The steps involved in monitoring
the effect of septic systems on
water quality will be among the
topics covered by representatives
of the University attending the
72nd annual meeting of the
American Society of Agronomy
now through Dec. 5 in Detroit.
In all, seven papers were
prepared for presentation by 10
members of the department of
soils and crops at Cook College.

Lowell Douglas and Warren
Busscher collaborated with Chen-
fang Lin, a graduate student, in the
preparation of the paper on septic
system monitoring. Some two-
and-a-half years of reasearch went
into the study.
"We found that probably 75
percent of the data in the literature
on this is in error because of a mis-
understanding of the way ground
water acts," said Douglas. "The

assumption was that it flows in a
straight line constantly. We found
the direction of flow to be, in fact,
quite variable."

This made the monitoring task
more difficult because of the high
number of observation wells that
had to be dug. For this reason, the
original group of some 30 in-
dividual household septic systems
being monitored shrunk to six as
the study continued.
"Also", said Douglas, "we
found that effluent from septic
systems does not ride on top of the
ground water, as had been
believed, but, rather, was stratified
within it. All in all, this study
showed that adequate monitoring
of a septic system is a much more
complex and expensive proposition
than anyone realized previously."
